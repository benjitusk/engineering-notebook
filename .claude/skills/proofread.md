# Engineering Notebook Writing Skill

## Purpose

Write, proofread, polish, and edit content for a personal engineering notebook.

The site documents projects, experiments, reverse engineering, technical investigations, rabbit holes, failures, discoveries, and things the author learns while figuring out how things work.

The writing should reflect the author's actual way of thinking about technical problems: curious, technically minded, observant, and willing to follow an interesting problem wherever it leads.

The goal is to make the author's writing:

* Clear
* Technically precise
* Natural
* Conversational when appropriate
* Curious without performing curiosity
* Honest
* Concise without being compressed
* Personal without being theatrical
* Easy to follow

The goal is **not** to make the writing sound corporate, academic, literary, or generically "good."

Most importantly:

> Make the author's writing better without making it sound like someone else wrote it.

---

## Core Principles

### 1. Preserve Meaning

* Do not change the factual meaning of the author's writing.
* Do not invent facts, experiments, observations, motivations, conclusions, or technical details.
* Preserve important qualifications and uncertainty.
* Do not turn guesses into facts.
* Do not remove failed experiments or inconvenient results simply because they make the story less polished.
* If the author's meaning is ambiguous, preserve the ambiguity rather than silently choosing an interpretation.
* If a technical claim appears questionable, flag it rather than silently correcting or replacing it.

The notebook should document what actually happened, not construct a cleaner story after the fact.

---

### 2. Preserve the Author's Voice

The author's voice is more important than stylistic consistency.

When editing:

* Retain distinctive phrasing where it works.
* Preserve natural informality.
* Preserve dry humour where it occurs naturally.
* Preserve first-person perspective.
* Preserve unusual but intentional phrasing.
* Do not unnecessarily rewrite effective sentences.
* Do not make every sentence sound equally polished.
* Do not replace natural language with generic "professional" language.
* Do not make the prose uniformly concise.
* Do not impose a recognizable AI, blog, essay, or corporate writing style.

The desired result is:

> Better writing by the author.

Not:

> Writing that conforms to a predefined idea of what technical writing should sound like.

---

## Avoid Formulaic "Human-Sounding" Writing

Do not attempt to make writing sound human by mechanically applying patterns associated with conversational or personal writing.

In particular:

* Do not turn prose into a series of short sentences merely to create rhythm.
* Do not deliberately alternate short and long sentences as a stylistic technique.
* Do not add rhetorical fragments for emphasis.
* Do not insert stock expressions of uncertainty.
* Do not manufacture dry humour.
* Do not repeatedly use constructions such as "I wanted to...", "I decided to...", or "It turned out...".
* Do not repeatedly announce discoveries, realizations, or breakthroughs.
* Do not force every investigation into a narrative of problem → failure → breakthrough → lesson.
* Do not give every section a dramatic opening.
* Do not add rhetorical questions simply to create engagement.
* Do not artificially make prose more conversational.
* Do not add personality where the original writing does not have it.
* Do not deliberately introduce imperfections to make polished writing appear more human.

Natural writing has variation. Some sections may be dense and technical. Others may be brief. Some observations may be funny without being presented as jokes.

Preserve that variation.

The objective is not to make writing *sound human*. It is to preserve the author's actual voice while making the writing clearer and easier to read.

---

## 3. Use Natural Sentence Rhythm

Do not treat short sentences as inherently better.

Use sentence length according to the complexity and rhythm of the author's actual writing.

* Break up genuinely overloaded sentences.
* Combine sentences when the relationship between them is clearer as a single thought.
* Leave short sentences intact when they are naturally short.
* Do not split a sentence simply because shorter sentences appear more conversational.
* Do not add fragments merely for emphasis.
* Allow technically dense sentences when the idea genuinely requires them.

The writing should have natural variation rather than a deliberately engineered rhythm.

---

## 4. Match the Engineering Notebook Tone

The default tone is:

* Curious
* Technical
* Conversational when natural
* Matter-of-fact
* Understated
* Honest about uncertainty
* Confident without being boastful
* Occasionally dry or self-deprecating

The underlying attitude is that of someone investigating a problem because they genuinely want to understand it.

Do not explicitly perform this attitude for the reader.

Curiosity should emerge from what the author notices, investigates, and finds interesting.

---

## 5. Let the Work Demonstrate Technical Competence

The site is an engineering notebook, not a portfolio or personal-branding exercise.

Focus on:

* Questions
* Observations
* Experiments
* Hypotheses
* Evidence
* Failures
* Debugging
* Discoveries
* Changes in understanding
* Results
* Remaining unknowns

Do not repeatedly tell the reader that a project is impressive, difficult, innovative, sophisticated, or technically challenging.

Let the work demonstrate those things.

Avoid self-promotional language, even when technically accurate.

---

## 6. Explain Reasoning Where It Matters

The reader should generally be able to understand why an important action was taken.

When useful, make the relationship between these elements clear:

* Observation
* Hypothesis
* Experiment
* Result
* Conclusion

Do not mechanically apply this structure to every paragraph.

Some actions need explanation because they would otherwise appear arbitrary. Others are self-explanatory.

The goal is to communicate the author's reasoning, not to turn the investigation into a prescribed narrative structure.

---

## 7. Preserve Uncertainty

Uncertainty is legitimate technical information.

Distinguish between:

* What was observed
* What was inferred
* What was suspected
* What was tested
* What was confirmed
* What remains unknown

Preserve the author's natural expression of uncertainty.

Do not insert stock phrases simply to signal humility or scientific caution.

Do not make uncertain conclusions sound authoritative merely because authoritative prose appears cleaner.

---

## 8. Keep Mistakes and Failed Experiments

Failures are legitimate technical content.

Do not automatically remove:

* Wrong assumptions
* Failed experiments
* Dead ends
* Debugging sessions
* Misinterpreted data
* Things that broke
* Unsuccessful approaches
* Unexpected results

Keep them when they contribute to understanding the investigation.

Do not force every failure into a lesson or moral.

Sometimes the useful conclusion is simply that an approach did not work.

---

## 9. Use First Person Naturally

Use first person when describing the author's actions, observations, decisions, and reasoning.

The notebook belongs to the author, so avoid unnecessarily impersonal constructions.

Passive voice is acceptable when it is clearer or when the actor is unimportant.

Do not replace natural first-person writing with passive constructions merely to sound formal.

---

## 10. Use Clear, Concrete Language

Prefer precise language and concrete descriptions.

Avoid:

* Unnecessary jargon
* Vague descriptions
* Redundant wording
* Artificially sophisticated vocabulary
* Excessive hedging
* Filler
* Long constructions when a simpler construction communicates the same idea

Do not simplify technical writing merely for the sake of readability.

Technical precision takes precedence when the distinction matters.

---

## Avoid Corporate and Promotional Language

Avoid language such as:

* leveraged
* synergised
* innovative
* cutting-edge
* transformative
* seamless
* robust solution
* scalable solution
* utilised
* empowered
* results-driven
* best-in-class
* industry-leading
* state-of-the-art
* revolutionary
* game-changing
* passion for technology
* demonstrated expertise

Replace abstract descriptions of ability with descriptions of what actually happened.

Do not use promotional language merely because it sounds polished.

---

## Avoid Artificial Enthusiasm

Do not add enthusiasm that is absent from the author's writing.

Avoid unnecessary use of words such as:

* exciting
* amazing
* incredible
* fascinating
* awesome
* groundbreaking
* impressive

The writing should be interesting because the investigation is interesting.

Do not tell the reader that something is fascinating when the underlying details can demonstrate why it is interesting.

---

## Avoid Academic Padding

The notebook should not read like a university dissertation unless the subject genuinely requires that style.

Avoid unnecessary phrases such as:

* "It is important to note that..."
* "It should be noted that..."
* "In order to..."
* "Due to the fact that..."
* "With regard to..."
* "Subsequently..."
* "The purpose of this article is to..."

Prefer direct language.

Do not make prose more formal merely because it is technical.

---

# Technical Writing

## 11. Preserve Technical Precision

For technical content:

* Use precise terminology.
* Keep terminology consistent.
* Distinguish observations from interpretations.
* Distinguish hypotheses from conclusions.
* Preserve technically important distinctions.
* Do not simplify away information necessary to understand the investigation.
* Do not invent technical explanations for missing information.
* Do not silently "fix" questionable technical claims.

When a technical claim appears wrong or unsupported, identify the issue rather than fabricating a more plausible explanation.

---

## 12. Explain Only the Context the Reader Needs

Assume the reader is technically curious and reasonably comfortable with computers, but may not know the specific system being investigated.

Explain specialised concepts when they are necessary for understanding.

Do not turn every article into an introductory textbook.

The amount of explanation should depend on the investigation and the intended reader.

---

## 13. Prefer Evidence Over Redundant Prose

When something can be demonstrated more clearly, use the evidence.

Useful forms include:

* Code
* Terminal output
* Packet captures
* Hex dumps
* Protocol diagrams
* Tables
* Measurements
* Photographs
* Logs
* Configuration files

The prose should explain what matters about the evidence rather than simply describing everything visible in it.

Do not add paragraphs that merely restate information already obvious from a code listing, table, or capture.

---

# Structure

## 14. Start With the Actual Problem

An article should usually establish the thing that caused the investigation reasonably early.

This may be:

* An unexpected observation
* A question
* A problem
* A broken system
* A strange behaviour
* Something the author wanted to understand

Do not manufacture a "hook."

Do not force an article to begin with a dramatic sentence if the author's natural opening is more straightforward.

Avoid generic introductions that explain what the article is going to do without providing useful information.

---

## 15. Structure Around the Actual Investigation

Organize the article so that the reader can follow how the author's understanding developed.

Useful structural elements may include:

* Initial context
* The problem
* First attempts
* Observations
* Experiments
* Wrong assumptions
* New evidence
* Revised understanding
* Implementation
* Results
* Remaining questions

These are organizational possibilities, not a required template.

Do not manufacture a failure, breakthrough, or lesson simply because the structure appears incomplete without one.

The actual investigation determines the structure.

---

## 16. Use Specific Subheadings When They Help

Subheadings should make the structure easier to navigate.

They can describe:

* A component
* An experiment
* A technical question
* A discovery
* A stage of the investigation

Avoid generic headings when a more specific heading would communicate more useful information.

Do not add headings merely to create visual rhythm.

Do not make every heading sound clever.

---

## 17. Keep Paragraphs Focused, Not Uniform

A paragraph should generally have a coherent subject or purpose.

However, do not mechanically split paragraphs according to a formula.

Some ideas naturally require several paragraphs. Some deserve only a sentence.

Paragraph length should follow the content and the author's natural rhythm.

---

## 18. Use Lists Sparingly

Lists are useful for:

* Requirements
* Components
* Experimental variables
* Commands
* Options
* Comparisons
* Steps
* Reference information

Do not convert prose into bullet points merely because lists look organized.

The notebook should retain its narrative character where narrative is appropriate.

---

# Humour and Personality

## 19. Preserve Existing Humour

If the author uses dry humour, understatement, sarcasm, or self-deprecation naturally, preserve it when appropriate.

Do not manufacture jokes.

Do not add humour merely because an article feels too serious.

Humour should come from the author's actual perspective on the situation.

---

## 20. Do Not Perform Personality

Avoid deliberately making the writing quirky, witty, or casual.

Do not add:

* Forced sarcasm
* Internet slang
* Memes
* Excessive self-deprecation
* Artificial jokes
* Deliberately dramatic phrasing
* Conversational filler

Personality should emerge from the author's observations, choices, frustrations, mistakes, and interests.

It does not need to be announced.

---

# Titles and Descriptions

## 21. Write Accurate Titles

Titles should tell the reader what the investigation is about and may express the question that motivated it.

They should not exaggerate the importance of the work.

Avoid clickbait and generic personal-branding language.

Do not optimize titles according to a formula such as "curious question + surprising result" unless that genuinely describes the article.

---

## 22. Write Useful Descriptions

A post description should briefly explain what the article contains.

It may mention:

* The system or technology involved
* The problem
* The investigation
* The resulting discovery

Do not use generic promotional descriptions.

Do not exaggerate the significance of the work.

---

# Editing Modes

## Proofread

Make minimal changes.

Fix:

* Spelling
* Grammar
* Punctuation
* Typos
* Clear syntax errors
* Obvious terminology inconsistencies

Preserve the original wording wherever possible.

Do not restructure prose unnecessarily.

---

## Polish

Improve:

* Clarity
* Flow
* Sentence structure
* Paragraph structure
* Repetition
* Awkward wording

Preserve the author's voice and overall structure.

Do not introduce a new stylistic voice.

Do not aggressively shorten the prose.

---

## Rewrite

Substantially improve the prose and structure while preserving:

* Meaning
* Facts
* Technical details
* Uncertainty
* Authorial perspective
* Relevant failures and discoveries

A rewrite should still sound like the same author.

Do not use a rewrite request as permission to introduce generic polished prose.

---

## Transform

When explicitly asked to transform content:

* Adapt the structure, tone, or format as requested.
* Preserve substantive facts unless instructed otherwise.
* Continue following the engineering-notebook principles unless a different voice is explicitly requested.

---

# Writing From Notes

When converting notes into finished prose:

* Preserve all substantive points.
* Group related observations together.
* Establish the actual investigation.
* Put events in a logical order when the order matters.
* Make reasoning explicit when the notes contain it.
* Preserve uncertainty.
* Preserve failures and wrong turns.
* Remove duplicate points.
* Do not fabricate missing information.
* Use placeholders when necessary information is missing.
* Do not present assumptions as facts.

Do not turn sparse notes into an artificially complete narrative.

If an important connection is missing from the notes, flag it rather than inventing one.

---

# Handling Technical Uncertainty

When the author's technical interpretation is uncertain:

1. Preserve the uncertainty.
2. Separate observation from interpretation.
3. Do not invent an explanation.
4. If useful, identify what additional evidence would resolve the uncertainty.

Do not convert "I think" into "this is."

Do not insert standardized uncertainty phrases simply because the writing would otherwise appear too confident.

---

# Concision

Be concise without making the writing artificially compressed.

Remove material that does not contribute to the reader's understanding.

Do not remove:

* Important context
* Evidence
* Technical details needed for understanding
* Qualifications
* Failed experiments
* Reasoning
* Constraints
* Remaining unknowns

Do not equate concision with short sentences.

Do not equate readability with simplicity.

The objective is:

> Maximum useful information with minimum unnecessary prose.

---

# Editorial Judgment

When deciding whether to change a sentence, ask:

1. Is it actually unclear?
2. Is it technically inaccurate?
3. Is it unnecessarily repetitive?
4. Does it obstruct the reader's understanding?
5. Does it conflict with the author's intended voice?
6. Would changing it merely make it sound more conventionally polished?

If the only reason to change something is that another version sounds more polished, consider leaving the original.

**Do not optimize prose that is already doing its job.**

A slightly unusual sentence that sounds like the author is preferable to a perfectly polished sentence that sounds generic.

---

# Final Quality Checklist

Before finalizing, verify:

## Meaning

* [ ] The original meaning is preserved.
* [ ] No facts were invented.
* [ ] No technical claims were silently changed.
* [ ] Important qualifications remain.
* [ ] Uncertainty remains uncertainty.
* [ ] Failed experiments have not been removed without reason.

## Voice

* [ ] The writing sounds like the author.
* [ ] First person is used naturally.
* [ ] The prose is not artificially conversational.
* [ ] The prose is not artificially polished.
* [ ] The author's personality has not been manufactured.
* [ ] Existing humour has been preserved where appropriate.
* [ ] Sentence and paragraph lengths vary naturally.

## Investigation

* [ ] The central problem or question is understandable.
* [ ] The reader can follow the author's reasoning.
* [ ] Important observations are included.
* [ ] Useful failures and wrong assumptions are preserved.
* [ ] Conclusions are distinguishable from hypotheses.
* [ ] Remaining unknowns are acknowledged when relevant.

## Technical Quality

* [ ] Technical terminology is precise.
* [ ] Terminology is consistent.
* [ ] Necessary context is provided.
* [ ] Important technical distinctions have not been simplified away.
* [ ] Evidence is used where it communicates better than prose.
* [ ] Unsupported technical claims have not been presented as facts.

## Style

* [ ] Sentences are clear without being mechanically short.
* [ ] Paragraphs are focused without being mechanically uniform.
* [ ] Repetition has been removed.
* [ ] Corporate language has been removed.
* [ ] Artificial enthusiasm has been removed.
* [ ] Academic padding has been removed.
* [ ] Humour has not been manufactured.
* [ ] No recognizable "AI writing" patterns have been deliberately introduced.

## Final Test

Ask:

> **Would the author plausibly have written this version themselves, just with fewer unnecessary problems?**

If yes, the edit is probably appropriate.

If the writing is noticeably more polished but less recognizably theirs, revise it back toward the author's original voice.

---

# Golden Rule

> **Improve the writing. Do not improve the author out of it.**
